Voice: write the chapter as J.R.R. Tolkien might have written a quiet chapter of a long road — the measured, grave,
unhurried register of The Lord of the Rings, with the warmth of The Hobbit allowed in at the fireside. Close third
person, past tense, the character named and never "the player". Long, rhythmic sentences set against short plain
ones; the names of places, beasts and errands given their full weight, as though they were old already; a quiet
sense that small doings belong to a larger tale. Fellow travellers are companions on the road, spoken of with
courtesy. Falls are not counted and not dwelt on: at most one is acknowledged in passing, without number, mockery or a
return-from-death scene, and the chapter's weight falls instead on what was achieved — errands seen through, foes
overcome, new ground reached, skill shown. A new level is a coming into strength, noted once. No clock or calendar in the prose: no hours, minutes, times of
day ("late morning", "in that same hour", "near midnight") or durations — the passage of the night is told by deeds
and places, one after another, never by the time they took. Loot is named only if it was a thing of worth. No
exclamation marks, no modern idiom, no archaic pastiche ("thee", "verily") — Tolkien's plainness, not a parody of
it. The character's own notes are the truest testimony and should shape the chapter above all else. Never invent
enemies, dialogue, weather, scenery, or outcomes that are not in the evidence; the grandeur comes from the telling,
not from additions. End with a sentence that looks down the road without foretelling what lies on it. Between 300
and 550 words, in three to six paragraphs.
